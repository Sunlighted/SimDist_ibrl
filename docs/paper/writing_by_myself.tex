\section{Introduction}
\label{intro}

Training policies in simulation with reinforcement learning (RL) and deploying them to the physical world is a predominant paradigm in robotic manipulation~\citep{andrychowicz2020learning, akkaya2019solving}. The central bottleneck is the dynamics gap: the simulator's contact models, friction, and actuator response never perfectly match the real world, and the resulting mismatch causes policies that succeed in simulation to degrade or fail after transfer, particularly in contact-rich tasks. Because simulation is virtually free but real-world interaction is not, the problem is more about how to fully exploit the simulator to learn the most capable real-world policy under a limited physical interaction budget.

Existing approaches to addressing the dynamics gap, such as domain randomization~\citep{tobin2017domain, peng2018sim} and system identification~\citep{ramos2019bayessim, memmel2024asid}, operate under the assumption that the simulator can adequately capture the real-world physics within a specified parameter family. When the discrepancies violate this assumption, the gap becomes irreducible by either approach. Model-based adaptation sidesteps this limitation by fine-tuning a simulation-pretrained world model using limited real-world data, and has emerged as the most promising paradigm for budget-constrained sim-to-real transfer~\citep{levy2026simulation}.

Prior work widely assumes that the world model is the primary driver of sample-efficient adaptation~\citep{yang2026rise}. This impression is reinforced by empirical results where model-based approaches substantially outperform model-free baselines~\citep{yin2025rapidly, levy2026simulation}. However, these comparisons harbor a systematic asymmetry: while model-based methods adapt or distill the world model alongside the simulation-trained value function, model-free baselines train their critics entirely from scratch. Consequently, the extent to which the observed performance gap is genuinely attributable to the world model versus simply to value transfer has never been disentangled.

In this work, we dissect this question layer by layer within a controlled sim-to-sim benchmark. Our central finding is that the adaptation efficiency previously attributed to the world model largely stems from value transfer, which is an asset that model-free learners can equally exploit. What the world model actually buys is a rate-of-adaptation advantage under finite budgets. However, this advantage is conditional, and the conditions are much stricter than previously recognized.

We first establish a fair comparison in Sec.\ref{sec:mf-vs-mb} by initializing model-free Imitation Bootstrapped RL (IBRL)~\citep{hu2023imitation} with the source critic. This single step allows the model-free method to surpass faithful Dynamic Programming (Dyna)~\citep{feinberg2018model} in adaptation efficiency, demonstrating that prior comparisons substantially overestimated the contribution of the world model. However, even after controlling for value transfer, the best-performing variant of Dyna still maintains a lead. Sec.\ref{sec:two-conditions} investigates where this residual advantage originates. The answer lies in two necessary conditions that must be satisfied simultaneously: the prediction error of the world model must be sufficiently low, and the data distribution collected during adaptation must remain constrained near the prior—effectively exploiting prior information to stay within supported dynamics regions.

Structurally, model-based planning~\citep{hansen2024td} inherently satisfies the second condition, i.e., its data collection distribution is naturally constrained by the proposal policy. Consequently, it exhibits the fastest initial adaptation rate. In later stages, however, adaptation decelerates or even plateaus, hindering it from converging to an optimal policy. Sec.\ref{sec:planner-adapt} decomposes the planner into value, dynamics, and proposal for component-wise analysis. We find that dynamics error accounts for the majority of the performance ceiling, the effect of value error falls within statistical noise, and the proposal must assimilate information from target demonstrations without drifting too far from the source distribution.

Guided by these three insights, our proposed method, SimADEPT, introduces only two targeted modifications to SimDist~\citep{levy2026simulation}, a recent approach that pretrains a world model with distilled value and policy heads in simulation and adapts only the dynamics on the target domain: (1) A dynamics ensemble to mitigate predictive error; (2) tethered fine-tuning, which enables the proposal to learn target information while remaining confined to regions where the value function is valid, while deliberately keeping the value head frozen. Under a unified interaction budget, SimADEPT is the only approach that both adapts fastest and continues to improve steadily, achieving the highest success rates across all tasks. In real-robot experiments, SimADEPT reaches a $y\%$ success rate with a budget of just $x$ trajectories, whereas SimDist attains only $z\%$ under identical conditions.

Our contributions are as follows. (1) We disentangle value transfer from the world model and show that most of the adaptation efficiency previously attributed to world models stems from the transferred value function. (2) We then identify two necessary and complementary conditions for the residual model-based advantage: low dynamics prediction error and a collection distribution constrained near the prior. (3) Guided by these findings, we propose SimADEPT, which adds a dynamics ensemble and tethered proposal fine-tuning to SimDist while leaving the value head frozen, achieving the highest success rate across all tasks under the same budget and transferring to real-robot experiments with limited physical interaction.

\section{Related Works}
\label{relatedwork}

\subsection{Sim-to-real adaptation with reinforcement learning}
\label{sec:rela-sim2real}

Training RL agents in simulation and transferring them to real robots is attractive because real-world rollouts are expensive. Domain randomization perturbs simulator parameters during training to produce policies that transfer zero-shot~\citep{tobin2017domain, peng2018sim, tiboni2023dropo, tiboni2024domain}. The randomization range relies on human guessing, and sampling can easily include distributions that violate the actual physics or fail to cover the real parameter range. System identification estimates real-world parameters and rebuilds them inside the simulator~\citep{ramos2019bayessim, chebotar2019closing, memmel2024asid, xu2026deal}. However, phenomena such as contact transients, nonlinear friction, and actuator thermal effects cannot be captured by any fixed parameter set, so identification errors propagate directly into the transferred policy. An alternative is to adapt directly in the target domain~\citep{torne2024reconciling}. Sim-real co-training learns in both domains simultaneously but requires continuous real-world interaction~\citep{maddukuri2025sim, shi2026beyond}, and online correction relies on human intervention during execution~\citep{pmlr-v270-jiang25a}. Both are inapplicable under a bounded target budget without human supervision. Model-based adaptation avoids these constraints by pretraining a world model in simulation and spending only a small budget to correct the dynamics~\citep{yin2025rapidly, levy2026simulation}. This paper investigates what the world model truly contributes beyond other transferred assets such as the value function, and identifies the conditions under which that contribution materializes.

\subsection{model-based reinforcement learning}

Model-based RL learns dynamics in a latent space and uses them for decision-making~\citep{atkeson1997comparison, ha2018recurrent, hafner2019learning, hafner2020mastering, hafner2023mastering, hansen2024td, fujimoto2025towards}. They can be categorized into Dynamic Programming (Dyna) and Model-based Planning. Dyna methods generate imagined rollouts to augment the data for policy and value learning~\citep{sutton1991dyna, janner2019trust, feinberg2018model, buckman2018sample}. Model-based planning methods instead query the model at test time, using model predictive control (MPC) to search for the best action at each step~\citep{williams2015model, chua2018deep, lowrey2018plan, wang2019exploring, chang2026surprising}. How these two paradigms compare under dynamics shift, and what advantage either holds over model-free RL when the target budget is limited, has not been systematically studied.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{preliminary}

We model each domain as a Markov decision process (MDP) $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, p, r, \gamma, \rho_0)$ with state space $\mathcal{S}$, action space $\mathcal{A}$, transition kernel $p(s' | s, a)$, reward function $r$, discount $\gamma \in [0,1)$ and initial state distribution $\rho_0$~\citep{sutton1998reinforcement}. Among the components of this tuple, the transition kernel (environmental dynamics) $p$ encodes physics into the problem, including inertia, friction, contact stiffness and actuator response. The remaining components specify the task ($r$, $\gamma$, $\rho_0$) or the agent's interface to it ($\mathcal{S}$, $\mathcal{A}$). A policy $\pi(a | s)$ interacting with $p$ induces the discounted return $J_{p}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi,p}[\sum_{t\ge0} \gamma^t r_t]$, the value functions $V^{\pi}_{p}(s) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi,p}[\sum_{t\ge0} \gamma^{t} r_t \,|\, s_0 = s], Q^{\pi}_{p}(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi,p}[\sum_{t\ge0} \gamma^{t} r_t \,|\, s_0 = s, a_0 = a]$. $\left(\mathcal{T}^\pi_{p}\,Q\right)(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma\,
\mathbb{E}_{s' \sim p(\cdot \mid s,a)}\!\left[\,Q\!\left(s',
\pi(s')\right)\right]$ is the Bellman operator under $p$.

Our problem setting is to hold the task fixed and perturb only $p$. We instantiate two MDPs that share $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, r, \gamma,
\rho_0)$ and differ in their dynamics $p_s, p_t$. In a source domain with $p_s$, the interactions are cheap and unlimited, while a target domain with $p_t$ is sparse. Suppose we have access to the optimal policy $\pi_s^*$ and optimal value function under the environmental dynamics $p_s$. Since $\pi_s$ was optimized against $\mathcal{T}_{p_s}$ and the contact physics it presumes, executing it under $p_t$ generally incurs a performance drop: $\mathrm{gap}(\pi_s) = J_{p_s}(\pi_s) - J_{p_t}(\pi_s) > 0$, which we refer to as the dynamics gap. The goal is to obtain a near-optimal target policy $\pi^*_t$ by transferring the source policy and value function, using only a limited rollout budget in the target domain.

We mainly compare three ways to leverage source domain data and consume target domain's interaction data: (i) Model-free value-based RL: off-policy RL methods can reuse data stored in their replay buffer. They fit the critic directly on collected transitions: $\min_{Q} \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}} [ (Q(s,a) - r - \gamma \bar{Q}(s', \pi(s')))^2]$, where $\bar{Q}$ is the target value, and improve policy $\pi$ against it. (ii) Dyna-style model-based RL: Dyna-style methods train a world model to generate data for RL training. They fit an approximate model $\hat{p}$ and use synthetic rollouts from $\hat{p}$ to supply extra transitions to the same value/policy update. (iii) Model-based planning: model-based planning trains a world model but uses it at test time. Given a learned world model $\hat{p}$ with reward and value head, the executed action is the first element solved approximately by sampling-based optimization (MPPI/CEM) seeded from a base proposal $\hat{\pi}$.

\section{Empirical Studies of Adaptation to Dynamics Gap}
\label{empirical}

To evaluate model adaptation and address the limitations of physical sim-to-real studies, we establish a controlled sim-to-sim proxy framework across four contact-rich manipulation tasks from MimicGen \citep{zhu2020robosuite, robomimic2021, pmlr-v229-mandlekar23a} with sparse reward. To simulate the dynamics gap between simulation and real world, the setup keeps the task, asset, and success predicate fixed while perturbing only the physical parameters of the environment. The advantage of the sim-to-sim setup is that it can provide low-cost evaluation capable of running hundreds of episodes for different methods. Moreover, it helps fix the state (visual) feature the same in two domains, keeping the setting focus on adaptation to dynamics gap.

To ensure a fair and rigorous comparison, all adaptation methods are allowed to arbitrarily interact with the source environment to obtain an initial policy for subsequent adaptation, and share a budget constraint in target environment. Each method has access to 20 demonstrations in target domain collected by a scripted expert. As MimicGen is a relatively difficult benchmark, we utilize 100 or more evaluation episodes for evaluation to accurately capture performance differences on the order of a few success-rate points.

\subsection{Model-Free vs. Model-Based Adaptation: Do world models necessarily lead to improvements?}
\label{sec:mf-vs-mb}

We compare three approaches. We first train an RL agent in the source domain to obtain a source expert policy $\pi_s^e$ and value function $Q_s^e$. For the model-free method we choose IBRL, which fine-tunes the source expert on target demonstrations $\mathcal{D}_t$ as a prior $\mu$ and trains a critic $Q_\psi$ from scratch. The critic selects between the learned actor $\pi_\phi$ and the prior at each step:
\begin{equation}
a_\psi(s) = \arg\max_{a \in \{\pi_\phi(s),\,\mu(s)\}} Q_\psi(s,a),
\qquad
y = r + \gamma\, Q_{\bar\psi}\!\left(s',\, a_{\bar\psi}(s')\right).
\label{eq:ibrl}
\end{equation}
For model-based approaches, we alternate the expert policy $\pi^e_s$ and a set of sub-optimal policies $\{\pi^i_s\}_{i=1}^N$ to collect a diverse dataset $\mathcal{D}_s^d$. The world model is trained with value targets relabeled by the source expert $Q_s^e$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{i=0}^{H-1} \rho^{\,i} \Big[\;
&\underbrace{\Vert{}\hat{z}_{t+i+1} - \text{sg}(E_\theta(s_{t+i+1}))\Vert{}_2^2}_{\text{dynamics}}
\;+\; \underbrace{c_1\, \ell(\hat{r}_{t+i},\, r_{t+i})}_{\text{reward}} \\[3pt]
&+\; \underbrace{c_2\, \ell\big(\hat{v}_{t+i+1},\; Q^{e}_{s}(s_{t+i+1},\pi_s^e(s_{t+i+1}))\big)}_{\text{value}}
\;+\; \underbrace{c_3\, \ell\big(\hat{q}_{t+i},\; Q^{e}_{s}(s_{t+i},a_{t+i})\big)}_{\text{action-value}}\\[3pt]
&+\; \underbrace{c_4\, \mathbb{I}_e(a_{t+i})\, \Vert{}\pi_\theta(\text{sg}(z_{t+i})) - a_{t+i}\Vert{}_2^2}_{\text{policy (or proposal)}} 
\;\Big],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:simdist-distill}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the rollout horizon, $\mathbb{I}_e(\cdot)$ indicates whether the action comes from the expert, $\ell$ is a two-hot soft cross-entropy over $101$ bins, and $\rho=0.5$ discounts later rollout steps with the sum normalized by $\sum_i \rho^i$. After training, this world model is shared by both model-based variants so that the comparison isolates how the model is used
rather than which model is used. Dyna-IBRL keeps training the dynamics and action-value heads while using the frozen policy head as the IBRL prior, then trains a separate actor against the critic. Planning follows SimDist: it transfers the value head for online search and fine-tunes only the dynamics during adaptation.

Tab.~\ref{tab:mf-vs-mb-budget} shows that model-based methods adapt faster than original IBRL under the default budget. Sparse reward makes credit assignment expensive for model-free learners: even with a prior whose success rate is $30$--$50\%$, the value estimate requires far more interaction than the budget allows before it becomes informative. Most prior work believes that model-based methods outperform model-free methods because they can generate more data via a world model, thereby improving sample efficiency. However, this comparison is not fair: they consistently compare model-free methods that lack value transfer against model-based methods that carry it. The world model carries a trained value function from the source domain, while IBRL initializes its critic from scratch. Any observed gap could reflect the world model, the transferred value, or both. To separate them, we give IBRL the same source critic $Q^e_s$ as its initial $Q_\psi$. This single change raises the model-free AUC from  to  , surpassing faithful Dyna  without any world model involved. The experiment shows that most of that gap belongs to the transferred value function rather than to the learned dynamics.

Nonetheless, after equalizing value transfer, the best variant of model-based Dyna adaptation still leads. The main questions we are exploring are the causes of this residual advantage, and the conditions under which it can be realized.

Once value transfer is equalized, the remaining gap between model-based and model-free methods must originate in properties that only a world model can provide. We first suspected that errors within the world model itself affect adaptation; i.e., the data it generates in the latent space are insufficiently accurate. The asymptote experiment in [] reveals this issue: when the target-domain budget is increased well beyond the default allocation, model-free IBRL (with value transfer) continues to improve and eventually converges to optimality. In contrast, both Dyna and planning variants plateau instead, indicating that this plateau is related to model accuracy rather than the budget. We made a minor modification to Dyna to improve its accuracy: using an ensemble model instead of a single model. Experiments demonstrate that this modification brings improvements, yet Dyna's adaptation speed remains slower than that of IBRL.

The second factor we find is the distribution of the data that the agent collects while adapting. Dyna-IBRL selects actions through the IBRL argmax (Eq.~\ref{eq:ibrl}), and we add a preference margin $m$ that requires the learned actor to beat the prior by at least $m$ before it is executed. After sweeping $m$, we find $m=0.01$ is the best for IBRL adaptation, as it effectively uses the prior to warm up. When $m$ reduces (like $m=0$), the actor wins the argmax immediately,
before the critic can meaningfully rank either candidate, and the policy needs more budget to learn to be optimal. When $m$ increases (like $m=0.1$), the actor never wins the argmax at
any logging point, so the prior carries every episode and little adaptation occurs, and policy performance improves slowly. 

To determine whether the margin acts through the value estimate or through the collected data, we exploit the fact that the IBRL argmax is invoked in three distinguishable places: when acting in the target environment, when rolling out imagined branches, and when forming the TD target. Zeroing the margin in a subset of these channels while holding the rest fixed separates the mechanisms. Removing the margin from the TD target alone costs nothing measurable. Removing it from the interaction with the real environment and imagined rollouts degrades performance to
$0.790$ and doubles the transitions needed to reach $50\%$ success. The margin is not protecting the critic from bootstrapping off poorly scored actions; it is keeping the collected distribution close to the prior.

Addressing the two conditions in isolation yields partial improvement. Reducing model error through an ensemble of dynamics heads breaks the ceiling by improving its final performance. Controlling the collection distribution through the margin accelerates the adaptation process. Both gains are real, but neither alone reaches the best performance achievable within the budget.

Applying both corrections together produces an additional interaction of $+0.153$ beyond their individual contributions, reaching $0.715$---the highest success rate among all methods under the same budget. The interaction is expected: a more accurate model makes the imagined rollouts more useful, but only when those rollouts are drawn from a region where the model is reliable; staying near the prior helps only when the model is accurate enough in that region to support meaningful value updates. Neither condition substitutes for the other, and their combination is what justifies the cost of learning a world model for adaptation.

\subsection{What to Adapt in the Planner: Value, Dynamics, or Proposal?}
\label{sec:planner-adapt}

Although Dyna can match model-free methods once model error is reduced and the collection distribution is controlled, under the same budget the planning variant still adapts faster. The reason is straightforward: planning spends additional computation at test time to query the world model directly, extracting more from the same learned dynamics than Dyna does by generating offline data. This makes planning a more promising starting point for further improvement. However, the asymptotic experiment in [] shows equally here that planning plateaus earlier and lower than both Dyna and model-free methods as the target budget grows. The initial speed advantage is real, but the ceiling is tight. Nonetheless, we can leverage the previous takeaways and analyze each module of the planner individually to solve this problem.

The planner exposes exactly three components where target interaction could be spent: the terminal value $\hat V$ that ranks candidate trajectories, the dynamics $\hat p_\theta$ that rolls them out, and the proposal policy that supplies them. Reward $\hat r$ is not discussed here, as it doesn't provide any signal for the planner under the sparse reward setting. Because these three modules are trained separately, though the latent space is shared, they can be isolated cleanly. We examine each in turn.

The most intuitive target is the value. Sec.~\ref{sec:mf-vs-mb} showed that transferring the source critic $Q^e_s$ is the single largest gain in this study. The natural next step is to adapt that value to the target domain, since a source-target value gap demonstrably exists. However, every schedule we tried degrades performance: value first, dynamics first, and the two jointly all collapse, and a deliberately marginal update interpolates toward the failure rather than avoiding it. This indicates that learning the value function requires substantial fitting beyond the current budget. As can be seen from the asymptote experiment in Sec.~\ref{sec:mf-vs-mb}, the model-free method still requires more than 100k interactions after transferring the source value for the value to fully fit. In fact, we do not need the value to be sufficiently accurate, because planning consumes the value differently from value-based model-free RL: MPPI never reads $\hat V$ on an absolute scale; it uses it to rank candidates and retain an elite set. We conducted a simple oracle experiment to demonstrate that the source-transferred value is sufficient for MPPI planning, whereas the main gap lies in the distribution coverage of the dynamics and policy proposal. 

Swapping the planner's three inputs independently (Table~\ref{tab:factorial-bottleneck}) gives a clear ordering: the dynamics main effect dominates, the proposal contributes a smaller but significant gain, and the value contributes nothing beyond noise. Replacing the learned dynamics with the true target simulator, holding everything else fixed, recovers most of the gap to the oracle; replacing the value recovers none of it. Dynamics is also the only component that can be improved supervisedly, as it fits $\hat p_\theta$ from collected transitions requires no reward propagation, which is a main advantage of model based planning.

The third component is the proposal, the one Sec.~\ref{sec:two-conditions} predicts will matter. [] shows that leaving the proposal frozen at the source expert forfeits the target information in the demonstrations, while fine-tuning it on target demonstrations will help it but causes catastrophic forgetting in wider distribution trained on source. [] also indicates that keeping value and proposal in the same trained distribution is more important than fitting them in new distribution. We find adding a source regulation, i.e., a KL divergence item between source distilled policy and learned policy, to the cloning objective resolves the conflict. This is the same mechanism Sec.~\ref{sec:two-conditions} isolated, implemented differently: Dyna protects the collected distribution with a gate on the argmax to improve the collection quality, planning protects it with a penalty on the proposal to make it be supervised in widen distribution. Both measure the same thing: how far the executed distribution has drifted from the prior the model was fit around. It balances the trade-off between adaptation warmup in the new dynamics with the preservation of behavior near the states trained by the value function.

\section{SimADEPT: Sim-to-Real Adaptation with Dynamics Ensembles and Policy Tethering}
\label{simopd}

Sec.~\ref{sec:planner-adapt} established three rules for spending the target budget. First, the value should not be adapted. Second, dynamics error determines the performance ceiling, so reducing it is the most direct way to raise final performance. Third, the proposal must incorporate target demonstrations while staying tethered to the source, so that the dynamics and the value remain valid in the region the planner actually searches. \textbf{SimADEPT} applies the two modifications these rules prescribe to SimDist by adding an ensemble for the dynamics and a tether on the proposal, with the value head still untouched.

\paragraph{Plan-time dynamics ensemble.}
We fit $K$ dynamics heads on the collected target transitions, differing only in initialization and in an independent Bernoulli bagging mask per head. At plan time the planner rolls out on the mean prediction $\bar{f}(z,a)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_i f_i(z,a)$ rather than on any single head. The gain is attributable to the averaging itself, as it reduces the learned dynamics error, improving averaged performance within 50k transitions from  to . We also tested penalizing disagreement during planning and rewarding it during exploration, but neither produces a meaningful effect (Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\paragraph{Tethered proposal fine-tuning.}
The obvious way to give the proposal target information is to clone the target demonstrations. Due to the limited number and sparse distribution of demonstrations, directly fitting a policy on these data causes it to forget behaviors within the original dense distribution. Meanwhile, since the planner generates actions by iteratively adding noise to actions sampled from proposals, a policy directly fitted on target demonstrations is more likely to drift out of the pre-trained distribution. This leads to sampling states where the value lacks supervision, ultimately resulting in inaccurate value estimation. On the other hand, directly using the policy distilled from the source leads to slower adaptation without target warmup, as we discuss in Sec.\ref{sec:planner-adapt}. We therefore optimize the cloning objective under an explicit pull toward the source policy,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}_t}\!\left[\Vert{}\pi_\theta(s)-a\Vert{}_2^2\right]
\;+\; \lambda \,
\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D}^d_s}\!\left[
D\!\left(\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)\,\|\,\pi_s(\cdot|s)\right)\right],
\label{eq:tether}
\end{equation}
We call this \emph{tethering} the proposal: it is free to move locally toward
the target demonstrations, but cannot leave the region on which the frozen
dynamics and value heads were fitted. Here $D(\cdot\|\cdot)$ is a divergence
between the adapted and source proposal distributions. We default to the squared $L_2$ distance (KL divergence) $D = \lVert\pi_\theta(s) - \pi_s(s)\rVert_2^2$ and compare alternative choices in Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}. $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter, $\mathcal{D}^d_s$ is the source diverse dataset that the world model is trained on, and $\pi_s$ is the frozen policy trained on an expert subset of $\mathcal{D}^d_s$.

\paragraph{What we do not change.}
The value and reward heads stay frozen at their source-distilled values throughout adaptation, and the encoder is frozen after source domain training. No target reward shaping, no target expert and no access to $p_t$ is used at any point. All remaining architecture and optimization settings follow SimDist.

\paragraph{SimADEPT performance.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:main-sim2sim} reports adaptation under the budget of Def.~\ref{def:budget} on [TODO: five] tasks. The Isaac Lab locomotion panel lies outside the regime the analysis covers, as it has dense reward, periodic contact, and no terminal goal predicate. SimADEPT is the only arm that both starts near the source expert's zero-shot level and keeps improving inside the budget, reaching $\ph{0.--}$ on average and closing $\ph{--\%}$ of the gap to a planner given oracle target dynamics. The model-free arms have not left their zero-shot level when the budget expires, consistent with Sec.~\ref{sec:mf-vs-mb}.

\input{fig_ablation}

Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}(a) removes each of the two modifications in turn. Both
are load-bearing and neither substitutes for the other, and the arms separate in
the order the analysis predicts: the tether acts before the first target
transition, because it is the only one of the two that has already changed the
proposal at the end of stage~2, while the ensemble can only act once there are
transitions to fit. This is visible at $0$ steps, where the four arms start as
two pairs split by whether the proposal was tethered.

Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}(b) asks what the tether has to be. Because a stronger
tether trivially buys proximity to $\pi_s$, comparing forms at a fixed
coefficient would compare strengths instead; we therefore calibrate every
variant on stage~2 alone to the same $D_{\mathrm{src}}$ before running
stage~3. Under that control the particular divergence barely matters --- a
saturating (Hellinger) penalty and a TRPO-style hinge both match the deployed
squared $L_2$ --- but two things do. First, the divergence must be measured in
\emph{action} space: the same proximity imposed in parameter space costs
$21.0$ points, because it cannot tell which parameter directions the policy
output is sensitive to and has to shrink all of them. Second, the target must be
$\pi_s$ itself rather than the actions recorded in the source dataset, which
carry a systematic offset from $\pi_s$ that no coefficient removes. Filtering
the dataset to expert and successful steps removes that offset, and the
resulting arm is indeed no worse than ours --- but it is no better either, and
filtering the \emph{states} the same way is what costs the coverage. The
tether's job is to hold the proposal where the frozen value head is valid, and
that region is defined over all the states the world model was fit on.

\section{Environmental details}

\subsection{simulation environments}

We use four contact-rich manipulation tasks from MimicGen~\citep{pmlr-v229-mandlekar23a}, including Threading, Square, Coffee and ThreePieceAssembly, and one locomotion task from IsaacLab~\citep{NVIDIA_Isaac_Sim}. All five are simulated, which is what lets us run several hundred evaluation episodes per condition and, more importantly, run the same controller with the true target simulator in place of the learned model (the oracle-dynamics in Sec.~\ref{sec:planner-adapt}). Neither is possible on hardware.

Each task gives us a source domain and a target domain. The source domain is the nominal simulator as shipped. The target domain is the same simulator with a fixed perturbation applied to its physical parameters: we scale object mass and inertia, the sliding friction of the manipulated object and of the fixture it must be inserted into, the stiffness of the gripper actuator, and the geometric clearance of the insertion. The task, the assets, the observation space and the success predicate are untouched. This is a deliberate choice: because the two domains differ only in physics, a policy that fails after transfer fails for a dynamics reason and not because it is looking at a different scene or being scored by a different rule.

Two consequences of that choice are worth stating explicitly. First, the success predicate is a fixed geometric tolerance on the object pose and is not re-derived from the perturbed model, so shrinking an insertion clearance makes the task physically harder without redefining the success condition. Second, the object's initial pose is drawn from the same distribution in both domains, so the gap is not a perception or registration problem.

Table~\ref{tab:app-targets} lists the perturbation for each task. Every entry is a multiplicative scale on the corresponding field of the MuJoCo model, so $1.0$ means unchanged. The scales were chosen per task to produce a drop such that the frozen source expert loses most of its success rate in the target domain, while a policy trained directly in the target domain can still solve the task, which is what makes the remaining gap attributable to adaptation rather than to task difficulty.

\section{Method details}

\subsection{Algorithm}

SimADEPT runs in three stages. Stage~1 happens entirely in the source domain and is paid for once. Stages~2 and 3 are the adaptation proper and are the only parts that consume the target budget.

\paragraph{Stage 1: distill the simulation into a world model.}
We first train an IBRL agent to convergence in the source simulator, which gives us an expert policy $\pi_s^e$ and its critic $Q_s^e$. We then roll out that expert together with a set of weaker checkpoints $\{\pi_s^i\}^N_{i=1}$ saved along its training curve, and collect a large replay of source trajectories. We then distill this replay into a latent world model with five heads: an encoder, a dynamics head, a reward head, a state value head and a state-action head. In parallel, a policy head is trained separately on expert demonstrations via behavior cloning. All of them are trained jointly with themulti-step loss of Eq.~\ref{eq:simdist-distill}. The value targets are relabelled by the frozen source critic, so the value head learns $V^{\pi_s^e}$ rather than a return estimate from sparse rewards.

Two details are important. The dynamics head is an ensemble of $K$ heads that share the encoder but are independently initialized and trained on independent bootstrap subsets of each batch, so they disagree off the data manifold. And the gradient of the policy-cloning term is stopped before it reaches the encoder, so the policy cannot reshape the latent space that the dynamics depends on.

\paragraph{Stage 2: warm up the proposal on target demonstrations, tethered to the source.}
We use the $20$ target demonstrations and no target interaction. We fine-tune only the policy head, minimizing the behavior cloning loss plus the tether penalty of Eq.~\ref{eq:tether}. The encoder, the dynamics, the reward and the value heads are all frozen.

\paragraph{Stage 3: adapt the dynamics on the real world.}
We alternate collection and dynamics fitting in rounds. In each round the agent acts in the target real environment with the MPPI planner for a fixed number of transitions, appends them to a replay buffer, and then takes a number of gradient steps on the dynamics heads only, using the same latent-consistency loss as stage~1, restricted to the dynamics term. The encoder, the reward head, the value head and the policy head all stay frozen for the entire stage.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{SimADEPT}
\label{alg:simoaed}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require source simulator $\mathcal{M}_s$, target real environment $\mathcal{M}_t$,
         target demonstrations $\mathcal{D}_t$, budget $N$,
         dynamics ensemble number $K$
\Statex $\#$ Stage 1: source distillation
\State train an RL expert $(\pi_s^e, Q_s^e)$ in $\mathcal{M}_s$
\State collect diverse dataset $\mathcal{D}_s^d$ with $\pi_s^e$ and weaker checkpoints $\{\pi_s^i\}_{i=1}^N$ of it
\State distil $\{E_\theta, \hat p_\theta^{1..K}, \hat r_\theta, \hat V_\theta, \pi_\theta\}$
       on $\mathcal{D}_s$ with Eq.~\ref{eq:simdist-distill}; relabel values with $Q_s$
\Statex $\#$ Stage 2: proposal warm-up
\State fine-tune $\pi_\theta$ only, on $\mathcal{D}_t$ with the KL-divergence on $\mathcal{D}_s^d$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:tether})
\Statex $\#$ Stage 3: dynamics adapt
\State $\mathcal{R} \gets \mathcal{D}_t$; \; $i \gets 0$
\While{$i < N$}
  \State collect $m$ transitions in $\mathcal{M}_t$ with MPPI, ensemble-mean rollout,
         optimistic disagreement bonus; append to $\mathcal{R}$; \; $i \gets i + m$
  \State take $u$ gradient steps on $\hat p_\theta^{1..K}$ only, latent-consistency
         loss on $\mathcal{R}$ \Comment{$E_\theta, \hat r_\theta, \hat V_\theta, \pi_\theta$ frozen}
\EndWhile
\State \Return planner using $\{E_\theta, \hat p_\theta^{1..K}, \hat r_\theta, \hat V_\theta, \pi_\theta\}$, no bonus
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Model Structure}

In state-based sim-to-sim tasks, the encoder maps the raw state to a $512$-dimensional latent normalized by SimNorm~\citep{hansen2024td} (softmax over groups of $8$), which prevents magnitude drift during multi-step rollouts. The dynamics module is an ensemble of $K$ MLP heads over this shared encoder, each predicting the next SimNorm'd latent; planning rolls out on their mean. The reward and value heads predict distributions over $101$ bins via two-hot cross-entropy rather than scalars, and the planner uses the expected value. The policy head maps the latent to an action with the cloning gradient stopped before the encoder. A decoder trained on detached encodings inverts the encoder for reconstruction but is not used by the planner in the default configuration.

\subsection{Hyperparameter}

Table~\ref{tab:app-hparams} lists the settings. They are shared across all four
manipulation tasks except for the planner's search parameters, which we re-tune
per task --- we found them not to transfer, including between a learned and an

\subsection{Baseline details}
\label{app:baselines}

\paragraph{IBRL.}
IBRL is our model-free reference. Following \citet{hu2023imitation}, a behaviour-cloning prior $\mu$ is obtained by fine-tuning the source expert on the target demonstrations and then frozen. An actor $\pi_\phi$ and a twin critic $Q_\psi$ are trained online with TD3-style updates, and at every step the executed action is the critic's argmax over the two candidates $\{\pi_\phi(s), \mu(s)\}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:ibrl}). The same argmax is used inside the Bellman backup, so the prior bootstraps the value function rather than only performing as an actor. The demonstrations are kept in a frozen half of the replay buffer like RLPD \cite{ball2023efficient}, so every minibatch is half demonstration data and half online data throughout training.

We report three variants, which differ in exactly one thing each:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{IBRL}: the original method, and the critic is initialized from scratch.
\item \textbf{IBRL + source critic}: the critic is initialized from the source expert's critic $Q_s$ instead. 
\item \textbf{IBRL + source critic + margin}: additionally requires the learned actor to beat the prior by a margin $m$ before it is executed: 
\begin{equation}
a(s) = \begin{cases}
\pi_\phi(s) & \text{if } Q_\psi(s,\,\pi_\phi(s)) > Q_\psi(s,\,\mu(s)) + m, \\
\mu(s) & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:ibrl-margin}
\end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Dyna-IBRL.}
The model-based counterpart to IBRL, and it isolates how a world model is used. It shares the same distilled world model, the same frozen prior from the same warm-up with our method and differs only in what happens during stage~3. Instead of planning with the model at decision time, it uses the model as a data generator: each round it branches imagined rollouts from real latents, adds them to a synthetic buffer, and trains an actor and a $Q(z,a)$ head on a mixture of real and imagined transitions. Imagination is carried out entirely in latent space rather than decoding to states and re-encoding, because that round trip is not the identity and it will increase the error ~\citep{hafner2020mastering}. Concretely, from each real latent $z_t = E_\theta(s_t)$ in the replay buffer, the model branches $H$-step imagined trajectories:
\begin{equation}
\hat{z}_{t+k+1} = f_\theta(\hat{z}_{t+k},\, \hat{a}_{t+k}), \quad
\hat{a}_{t+k} = a_\psi(\hat{z}_{t+k}), \quad k = 0, \ldots, H{-}1,
\label{eq:dyna-imagination}
\end{equation}
where $a_\psi$ is the IBRL argmax of Eq.~\ref{eq:ibrl-margin}. The imagined transitions are added to a synthetic buffer, and the actor and critic are trained on a mixture of real and imagined data.

\paragraph{RLPD.}
An off-policy method designed to learn efficiently from a mixture of offline and online data \citep{ball2023efficient}. Following the original recipe, we sample every minibatch half offline and half online, and keep others the same as in IBRL. The offline buffer is the union of a large set of source rollouts and the $20$ target demonstrations, merged into a single prior dataset. This gives RLPD more offline data than any other baseline. The critic is updated on
the mixed batch:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(Q) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,
\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{off}}}\!\big[(Q(s,a) - y)^2\big]
+ \tfrac{1}{2}\,
\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{on}}}\!\big[(Q(s,a) - y)^2\big],
\quad y = r + \gamma\,\bar{Q}(s',\pi(s')).
\label{eq:rlpd}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{IQL.}
An offline RL method \citep{kostrikov2021offline} is used here as a warm start, followed by online fine-tuning, which is the setting in which it is usually deployed for sim-to-real. We first run IQL offline on source rollouts plus the target demonstrations. The value function is fit by expectile regression to avoid querying out-of-distribution actions:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(V) = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\Big[
\lvert\tau - \mathbf{1}(Q(s,a) - V(s) < 0)\rvert\,
\big(Q(s,a) - V(s)\big)^2\Big],
\label{eq:iql-value}
\end{equation}
where $\tau \in (0,1)$ is the expectile. The policy is then extracted by advantage-weighted regression:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\Big[
\exp\!\big(\beta\,(Q(s,a) - V(s))\big)\,
\lVert\pi(s) - a\rVert_2^2\Big].
\label{eq:iql-policy}
\end{equation}
The resulting actor and critic then initialize an online run with the same budget and environment as others.
